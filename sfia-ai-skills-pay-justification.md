# Compensation case: SFIA AI skills command a higher rate

**Purpose.** A working draft for a pay conversation. It reviews the [SFIA AI Skills Framework](https://sfia-online.org/en/tools-and-resources/ai-skills-framework) and turns it into a justification: why someone who actually holds these skills is underpriced if they are paid as a generic digital, software, or “AI-curious” practitioner.

**How to use it.** Keep the executive summary. Drop in the person’s demonstrated SFIA levels. Send the one-page memo. Keep the appendix for HR, a compensation committee, or a sceptical hiring manager.

**Source of truth.** SFIA 9 (Skills Framework for the Information Age), including Machine learning (MLNG), AI and data ethics (AIDE), and the SFIA mappings for CWA 18398 AI professional role profiles. Market figures are cited at the end and will move; the skills argument does not depend on any single survey.

---

## Executive summary

SFIA does not treat “AI skills” as one extra tool on a software engineer’s belt. It describes a **stacked professional capability**: decide where AI should and should not be used; build or select models; put them into production as engineered systems; keep them reliable, fair, private, and auditable; and redesign the human work around them.

That stack is scarce by SFIA’s own design. Literacy and tool use are meant to scale across the workforce. **Building models and operationalising AI/ML applications are specialised, high-impact work** that SFIA says requires advanced education, training, and specialised resources — and that **directly creates organisational IP, competitive advantage, and new products**.

The pay case is therefore not “AI is fashionable.” It is:

1. **The work is a different job**, not a software job with a chatbot. SFIA maps it across data science, machine learning, data engineering, software/systems design, MLOps, security, ethics, governance, and organisational change.
2. **The accountability is higher.** Production AI systems are non-deterministic. They drift, discriminate, leak data, fail silently, and can fall into high-risk regulatory categories. SFIA puts humans at Level 5 (“ensure and advise”) in the loop for a reason.
3. **The labour market already prices this.** PwC found workers with AI skills commanding a **56% wage premium** over people in the *same occupation* without those skills (2024, up from 25% the year before). US market data in 2026 still shows a clear ML/AI-engineering premium over general software engineering.
4. **Replacement is slow and expensive.** These skills change faster than neighbouring digital skills, take years to build, and cannot be hired off the shelf at generic-band rates without losing time, quality, or risk control.

If the person is operating at **SFIA Level 4 (Enable)** or **Level 5 (Ensure, advise)** across machine learning plus one or more of data engineering, MLOps, ethics, architecture, or transformation, they are doing work whose impact, scarcity, and downside risk sit above a standard professional-services or software band. Pay should follow the **level of responsibility and the width of the stack**, not the job title.

## 1. What the SFIA AI framework actually is

SFIA is an open, globally used professional-skills framework. Version 9 is the current release. The [AI skills view](https://sfia-online.org/en/tools-and-resources/ai-skills-framework) is not a vendor catalogue of tools. It is a way to describe **enduring professional capabilities** for working with AI, placed on SFIA’s seven levels of responsibility.

SFIA is explicit about the problem it is solving:

- AI technologies move too fast for a static tool list.
- Organisations need **breadth and depth** without inventing a siloed “AI profession” for every job.
- AI work has **ethical and legal implications** that generic digital skills do not cover.
- Different industries adopt AI differently, so the framework describes tasks and accountability, not products.

That last point matters for pay. A person who can move between platforms, models, and vendors — and still own outcomes — is more valuable than a person who can operate one current tool.

### Four capability layers (this is the whole argument)

SFIA groups AI-related work into four layers with **different scope and different impact**:

| Layer | Who it is for | What SFIA says about impact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. AI / data literacy | Broad workforce | Foundation. Necessary, not scarce. |
| 2. Use AI to automate, assist, augment | Digital, data, tech, cyber professionals | Productivity, quality, and decision quality across existing roles. |
| 3. Develop and operationalise AI/ML applications | A subset of specialists | **Critical** for implementation and maintenance; competitive advantage; reliability, security, scale. |
| 4. Build the AI/ML models | A specialised minority (data scientists, ML engineers, AI researchers) | **Requires advanced education, training, and specialised resources.** Creates IP, differentiation, new products, and solutions to complex business problems. |

Layers 1 and 2 are becoming table stakes. Layers 3 and 4 are the scarce supply. **Someone who can do 3 and 4, and also hold the ethics/governance work that SFIA added in SFIA 9, is in the thinnest part of the market.** Paying them as if they only sit in layer 2 is a category error.

SFIA also warns against “AI inflation”: not every role that touches AI is a model-builder. The converse is also true. If someone *is* building, deploying, assuring, and governing AI systems, collapsing that into “uses AI” is how organisations underpay the people who make AI safe to use.

## 2. Review: what “these skills” actually require

This section is the review. It is also the evidence pack. Each block is something SFIA already says a competent practitioner must be able to do.

### 2.1 Machine learning (MLNG) is not prompt use

[Machine learning](https://sfia-online.org/en/sfia-9/skills/machine-learning) is defined as *developing systems that learn from data and experience, improving performance, accuracy and adaptability in dynamic environments.*

The guidance notes include:

- judging whether machine learning is even the right approach
- selecting techniques, algorithms, and tools for a business problem
- data preparation, cleansing, transformation, feature engineering
- designing, training, optimising, and retraining models (supervised, unsupervised, reinforcement)
- MLOps: deployment, monitoring, lifecycle
- evaluating performance, robustness, fairness, and bias
- diagnosing failures before and after deployment
- anticipating ethics, privacy, sustainability, and data-protection implications
- establishing **traceability** for outcomes
- continuous / real-time adaptation as data and conditions change

SFIA does not define this skill at Level 1. It starts at Level 2 because the work already needs more autonomy and judgement than “follow instructions.” That is a pay-relevant fact: the floor of this skill is already above routine digital work.

**What the levels mean in practice**

- **Level 2 — Assist.** Helps with data prep, training, evaluation; builds basic models for well-defined problems under supervision.
- **Level 3 — Apply.** Applies established techniques to business problems; trains, validates, deploys, monitors; communicates results **and limitations**.
- **Level 4 — Enable.** Assesses suitability, designs solutions across a range of problems, guides others, engineers features, anticipates risks, integrates models into production, troubleshoots in depth.
- **Level 5 — Ensure, advise.** Leads solutions for **complex, high-impact** problems; architects end-to-end pipelines including MLOps; sets practices and standards; gives expert advice; aligns initiatives with organisational goals.
- **Level 6 — Initiate, influence.** Sets organisational ML strategy and roadmap; establishes governance for responsible, ethical, sustainable use; builds organisational capability; drives high-impact opportunities with senior stakeholders.

A Level 4 or 5 ML practitioner is already in “enable / ensure” territory: they design, they guide others, and they are accountable for outcomes, not tickets.

### 2.2 AI and data ethics (AIDE) is a new, high-floor skill

[AI and data ethics](https://sfia-online.org/en/sfia-9/skills/ai-data-ethics) is **new in SFIA 9**. It starts at **Level 3**, not Level 1. SFIA’s own rule: lower levels are omitted when the skill needs more autonomy, influence, and complexity than those levels provide.

The work includes fairness, accountability, transparency, privacy, bias, employment impact of automation, ethical impact assessments, incident response, and designing systems that embed ethics rather than bolting it on.

At Level 4 the person leads stakeholder conversations that change design decisions. At Level 5 they give expert advice, oversee assurance, and approve impact assessments. At Level 6 they set organisational direction and allocate resources for ethical assurance.

This is risk-bearing professional work. It is closer to a control function than to a productivity hack. Control functions are paid for the losses they prevent as well as the work they ship.

### 2.3 Operationalising AI is an engineering system, not a notebook

SFIA is blunt: **AI engineering / operations is not “just building models.”** Production AI applies:

- data engineering (training/inference pipelines, feature stores, lineage, DataOps)
- machine learning and data science
- systems design and software architecture (including fallback, override, human-in-the-loop)
- solution architecture across data, model, infrastructure, and monitoring
- lifecycle engineering (CI/CD for models, validation, rollback)
- infrastructure (GPU/compute, containerised inference, cost, resilience)
- supplier and sourcing management (because AI now arrives inside purchased software, APIs, and data supply chains)

The [operationalising view](https://sfia-online.org/en/tools-and-resources/ai-skills-framework/skills-for-developing-operationalising-ai-ml) stretches further still: strategy, architecture, governance, risk, business analysis, change management, security and assurance, quality, safety, financial appraisal, workforce design. That is not one job description. It is why a person who can *span* several of these is expensive. Most organisations need a team. A person who can cover a team-shaped gap is priced like the gap, not like a single job family.

### 2.4 Building models is explicitly specialised

The [model-building view](https://sfia-online.org/en/tools-and-resources/ai-skills-framework/skills-focused-on-building-ai-ml-models) names a small set of deep skills: data science (DATS), formal research (RSCH), emerging technology monitoring (EMRG), machine learning (MLNG), numerical analysis (NUAN), high-performance computing (HPCC).

SFIA’s impact statement for this layer is the compensation thesis in one paragraph:

- it **directly contributes to AI/ML capabilities and intellectual property**
- it **drives innovation and differentiation** through organisation-specific models
- it enables the organisation to **solve complex problems, generate insights, and develop new products and services**
- it **requires advanced education, training, and specialised resources**

That is not language SFIA uses for generic digital literacy. It is language used for scarce production capability.

### 2.5 Governance is a coordinated set of responsibilities, not a policy PDF

SFIA’s AI assurance material says governance is **not a single role**. It spans engineering, operations, change, governance, and independent audit. Skills include governance (GOVN), risk (BURM), quality assurance and management, AI and data ethics, and information/data compliance.

SFIA also flags generative-AI tools that draft job descriptions, screen candidates, or recommend learning paths as **high-risk worker-management systems**. Employers must show data quality, transparency, explainability, and bias controls. The person who can both build the system and evidence those controls is doing two jobs that the market usually splits — and that regulators will not let the organisation skip.

### 2.6 The European role map shows how wide “one AI professional” already is

SFIA’s illustrative mappings for **CWA 18398** AI professional role profiles are not job ads. They are a picture of the labour market SFIA thinks organisations need: data scientist, data engineer, MLOps engineer, AI architect, AI evaluator, observability specialist, incident-response lead, platform engineer, security specialist, safety specialist, responsible AI officer, transformation lead, governance officer, risk manager, auditor, product manager, and more.

A practitioner who can operate credibly across even **two or three** of those archetypes (for example: ML engineering + MLOps + evaluation, or architecture + ethics + transformation) is filling roles that other organisations hire as separate specialists. Combined capability is a premium, not a rounding error.

## 3. Why this skill set is worth more money

### Argument 1 — SFIA levels are a responsibility ladder, and pay follows responsibility

SFIA’s seven levels are defined by **autonomy, influence, complexity, knowledge, and behavioural factors** — not by years of service or tool names.

| Level | Essence | Why it costs more |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 Apply | Varied, sometimes complex work; discretion; own deadlines | Competent individual contributor. Market rate for the profession. |
| 4 Enable | Diverse complex work; guides others; autonomous; contributes expertise | Multiplies other people’s output. Designs, not only executes. Anticipates risk. |
| 5 Ensure, advise | Authoritative guidance; accountable from analysis through evaluation; significant outcomes | Own the result. Broad direction, not tasking. Expert advice that others act on. |
| 6 Initiate, influence | Shapes policy, strategy, organisational capability | Priced as leadership of a capability, not as an extra senior IC. |

Level 5, in SFIA’s words: works under **broad direction**; work is **self-initiated**; influences **critical decisions**; has **significant influence over resources**; performs an **extensive range of complex** work in **unpredictable** contexts; offers **authoritative advice**.

If the person is doing Level 4/5 AI work while being paid at a Level 3 digital band, the organisation is buying Level 5 accountability at Level 3 rates. That is a retention and ethics problem as well as a market problem: the downside of a bad model sits with the organisation, but the person carrying the judgement is not being paid for that judgement.

### Argument 2 — The failure modes are expensive in a way ordinary software is not

A conventional bug is usually reproducible. A model can be accurate in training, wrong for a subpopulation, silently degraded by drift, poisoned by a data-supply issue, or compliant yesterday and unlawful tomorrow.

SFIA’s ML guidance puts fairness, bias, privacy, traceability, and post-deployment diagnosis in the skill definition itself. Ethics starts at Level 3 because you cannot “follow a script” through an ethical incident.

The person with these skills is therefore paid for:

- **value created** (automation, new products, better decisions, IP)
- **value protected** (avoided regulatory action, discrimination claims, outages, security incidents, reputational damage, wasted model spend)

Compensation that only counts the first column undervalues the job.

### Argument 3 — Breadth across SFIA categories is the scarce unit, not a single skill code

SFIA’s own AI summary chart makes the point: the relevant skills are **scattered across categories**. Most roles need a combination of technical expertise, stakeholder management, strategic thinking, and delivery.

The market can find people who have used a model API. It struggles to find people who can:

- choose *not* to use ML when it is the wrong tool
- prepare and steward the data
- train and evaluate with robustness and bias in mind
- design the surrounding system (fallback, human override, audit trail)
- ship through MLOps with monitoring and rollback
- explain limitations to non-technical stakeholders
- sit in the governance conversation without being decorative

That combination is what SFIA is describing. It is also what takes years, not a course.

### Argument 4 — The organisation captures the upside of a skill that decays unusually fast

SFIA leads with pace of change as the first challenge in defining AI skills. PwC’s 2025 Global AI Jobs Barometer found skills sought by employers changing **66% faster** in the occupations most exposed to AI than in the least exposed (up from 25% the year before). The 2026 barometer found skills in the most AI-exposed jobs still changing **more than twice as fast**, and new tasks **2.5× more likely** to rely on judgement, empathy, and creativity.

The employer gets:

- current methods without a long internal academy
- someone who can re-skill the work as the stack moves
- a translator between research, engineering, risk, and the business

Continuous re-skilling is unpaid overtime the organisation banks as capability. A higher rate is how you keep that person from taking the capability next door.

### Argument 5 — SFIA says these people change other people’s jobs, not only their own

The automate / assist / augment layer plus **job analysis and design (JADN)** is about redesigning work: what AI does, what stays human, and what a Level 3 “apply” activity may be delegated to a system while a Level 5 human remains accountable.

Someone who can re-engineer processes around AI is a **force multiplier**. Their output is measured in other teams’ capacity, error rates, cycle time, and risk posture — not in tickets closed. Force-multiplier roles are priced above the roles they multiply. That is already how organisations treat strong tech leads, architects, and security leads. AI capability of this depth is the same pattern.

### Argument 6 — Independent market evidence already shows a premium

These figures will move. They are here to show that the SFIA argument is not a private theory.

- **PwC 2025 Global AI Jobs Barometer** (nearly 1 billion job ads): workers with AI skills commanded an average **56% wage premium** in 2024 versus people in the *same occupation* without those skills, up from **25%** the year before. The premium appeared in **every industry analysed**. Jobs requiring AI skills grew **7.5%** while total postings fell **11.3%**. Industries most exposed to AI saw **3×** higher growth in revenue per employee (27% vs 9%).
- **PwC 2026 AI Jobs Barometer**: productivity growth **40% higher** at the most AI-exposed companies versus the least; a two-track market in which “professionalised” AI jobs grow twice as fast as “democratised” ones, with **42% faster wage growth** since 2021. This is the SFIA split in labour-market form: layer 2 gets cheaper; layers 3–4 get more expensive.
- **US 2026 compensation snapshots** (indicative, not a local salary survey):
  - Levels.fyi: Machine Learning Engineer median total compensation about **$280k** vs Software Engineer about **$195k** (~**$85k / 44%** gap).
  - Robert Half 2026 midpoint: AI/ML Engineer base about **$170,750** vs Software Engineer **$142,000** (~**20%**).
  - Robert Half: **49,200** US AI/ML/data-science postings in 2025, **up 163%** year on year; **64%** of technology leaders said AI integration projects were delayed by skills shortages.
  - Ravio 2026: AI/ML hiring **+88%** year on year, with a reported **12%** professional/IC salary premium versus non-AI peers.

Local pay will differ by country, sector, and public vs private. The direction is consistent: **the market already pays more for the specialised layers SFIA isolates.** An employer that does not will lose people to employers that do.

### Argument 7 — Substitution is a false economy

Common counters, and the SFIA-shaped replies:

**“Everyone uses AI now.”**  
Everyone is supposed to use layer 1–2. SFIA created separate views for operationalising and model-building because those are not the same skill, not the same training investment, and not the same impact.

**“It’s just software engineering.”**  
Software design and programming are *inputs*. SFIA adds data science, ML, numerical methods, HPC, drift monitoring, fairness evaluation, ethical impact assessment, model traceability, and human-in-the-loop design. The failure mode is statistical and socio-technical, not only logical.

**“A junior with Copilot can do this.”**  
MLNG is not defined at Level 1. AIDE is not defined at Level 1 or 2. SFIA is telling you the judgement required is above “follow a procedure.” Copilot increases the output of someone who already has the judgement. It does not create the judgement.

**“We’ll buy a vendor platform.”**  
SFIA puts sourcing and supplier management inside the AI engineering picture precisely because purchased software now embeds AI. Someone still has to assess hidden models, data supply chains, contractual liability, security, and whether the organisation is comfortable delegating a given SFIA-level activity to a black box.

**“We can’t pay above band.”**  
Then the band is wrong for the work. SFIA is a ready-made levelling language for compensation committees: map the role to skills and levels, then place it against equivalent Level 5 work in security, architecture, or risk — not against a Level 3 developer who has tried ChatGPT.

### Levelling rule of thumb for pay

- **Mostly Level 3, one domain:** professional rate for that job family, plus a modest AI-skill premium (the PwC “same occupation, with AI skills” pattern).
- **Level 4 across two or more of: ML, data engineering, MLOps, ethics:** senior specialist / lead rate. This person enables others and owns design choices.
- **Level 5 in ML or architecture, plus credible ethics/governance:** principal / expert-advisor rate. This person is accountable for significant outcomes and is the organisational backstop.
- **Level 6:** capability-owner / Head of / CAIO-adjacent rate. Strategy, policy, and organisational ML adoption sit here.

### What “higher pay” should mean in the conversation

Pick one, named, measurable ask. Examples:

- Re-band the role from “software engineer / analyst” to “ML / AI engineer” or “AI assurance + engineering,” with the matching salary range.
- Apply a **specialist market premium** of X% on the current band, justified by the PwC same-occupation premium and local market data.
- Promote on responsibility (SFIA 4 → 5), not on tenure, because the work already matches “ensure and advise.”
- Separate **variable pay** tied to production AI outcomes and risk incidents avoided, so the organisation is honest that this is high-leverage work.

Do not argue from hours worked. Argue from **level of responsibility, width of stack, scarcity, and cost of getting it wrong.**

**To:** [Manager / HR / compensation committee]  
**From:** [Name]  
**Re:** Market and responsibility case for AI-skilled compensation  
**Date:** [Date]

I am asking that [my / this] compensation be set against the **specialised AI/ML capability** described by SFIA 9, not against a generic digital or software band.

SFIA distinguishes AI literacy and everyday tool use from two scarce layers: **building machine learning models** and **operationalising AI/ML systems in production**, plus the **ethics and governance** work SFIA added because AI systems create fairness, privacy, safety, and regulatory exposure. Model-building is described as specialised work that requires advanced training and that creates organisational IP and new products. Operationalising is described as critical to competitive advantage and to reliability, security, and scale.

[I operate / this role operates] at approximately **SFIA Level [4/5]** in [MLNG / DATS / DENG / …], evidenced by [two or three concrete outcomes: systems in production, standards written, incidents handled, value delivered, risk avoided].

That is “enable” or “ensure and advise” work: designing solutions, guiding others, owning outcomes from analysis through evaluation, and advising the organisation on techniques, limitations, and risk. It is not Level 3 application of a chatbot.

The labour market already prices this gap. PwC’s 2025 Global AI Jobs Barometer found a **56% average wage premium** for AI-skilled workers versus the same occupation without those skills. US 2026 data still shows ML engineering paid materially above general software engineering, and employers report delayed AI programmes because they cannot hire the skill.

Underpaying this capability has a clear cost: slower delivery, weaker models, unowned drift and bias, fragile production systems, and retention risk for the people who can actually put AI into service without creating a governance incident.

## 6. Framework review — strengths, limits, and how not to misuse it

**What SFIA is strong for in a pay case**

- It is independent, open, and used by employers, governments, and professional bodies. It is not a personal branding document.
- It separates **responsibility level** from **skill**, so you can show that the same skill at Level 5 is a different job from Level 3.
- It refuses tool-chasing. That protects the case when the current model vendor changes.
- It makes ethics, governance, and “what stays human” first-class, which is where a lot of the organisational risk (and therefore value) sits.
- The CWA 18398 mappings give an external role language if someone says “that isn’t a real job.”

**What SFIA is not**

- It is not a salary survey. Levels do not translate to a currency figure by themselves.
- It is not a licence. Mapping yourself to Level 6 without organisational influence will collapse in the room.
- It is not an argument that everyone who uses GenAI should be paid 56% more. PwC’s premium is for roles that **require** AI skills; SFIA’s scarce layers are 3 and 4.
- Mappings are starting points. SFIA says organisations must tailor to operating model, AI maturity, sector, and risk.

**The cleanest way to use it**

Describe the work in SFIA verbs and levels. Then attach **local** market data and **this organisation’s** cost of delay, incident, and replacement. Framework first, survey second, personal evidence third.
